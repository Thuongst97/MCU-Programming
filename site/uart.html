USART: Frames, Interrupts and a Command Shell

A UART turns bytes into a timed sequence of high and low levels on one wire, and back. No clock line is shared, so both sides must agree on the timing in advance. This module covers the frame, the baud-rate divider, interrupt-driven I/O with a lock-free ring buffer, a small command shell, and the LIN mode .

8.1Anatomy of a UART frame

The line idles high. A frame starts with one low start bit, then 5–9 data bits least significant bit first, an optional parity bit, and one or two high stop bits. "8N1" means 8 data bits, no parity, 1 stop bit: 10 bit times per byte. Type a character and watch its frame:

The receiver detects the falling edge of the start bit, then samples each bit in its middle. A G4 USART normally oversamples by 16 and takes three samples around the middle of each bit to reject noise; disagreeing samples set the noise flag NE (RM0440, USART). The LPUART, built to run from a slow clock in low-power modes, uses its own fractional divider instead (below).

🎭
Analogy

Two people agree to tap out a message at exactly one tap per second, without looking at each other's watch. The first tap says "starting now"; after that each listener counts seconds on their own watch. If one watch runs 5 % fast, by the tenth tap they are half a tap apart and start hearing the wrong taps. That is why both sides need accurate clocks.

8.2Baud rate and BRR

Each serial peripheral divides its kernel clock (RCC_CCIPR, by default the bus clock) down to the baud rate. The G4 has two flavours with different formulas (RM0440, USART and LPUART):

USART (16× oversampling): BRR = fck ÷ baud  ·  LPUART: BRR = 256 × fck ÷ baud

The ST-LINK's virtual COM port is wired to LPUART1, so every log line in the course goes through the second formula: 256 × 170 MHz ÷ 115 200 = 377 777.8 → 0x5C3B2. The LPUART register has 20 bits and must hold at least 0x300, so very low baud rates need a slower kernel clock.

USART1 is on APB2; USART2/3, UART4/5 and LPUART1 are on APB1. At the course setup both buses run at 170 MHz; after a change to a prescaler they would not. Plugging in the wrong clock, or the USART formula for an LPUART, is the classic cause of garbage output. The receiver tolerates a total clock mismatch of a few percent between the two sides; the calculator shows the error that the divider's rounding adds.

8.3Polling vs interrupts

Polling waits on the status flags: simple, but the CPU can do nothing else, and at 115 200 baud a byte takes about 87 µs. Interrupts let the USART call you when it needs attention:

Flag (USART_ISR)Interrupt enable (USART_CR1)Means
RXNERXNEIEA received byte waits in RDR. Reading RDR clears it.
ORERXNEIEOverrun: a new byte arrived before the old one was read. The new byte is lost. Clear it by writing ICR.ORECF; until you do, no further byte is received.
TXETXEIETDR is free for the next byte. Stays set while empty, so disable TXEIE when you have nothing to send.
TCTCIEThe last bit has left the pin. Needed before turning the USART off or switching an RS-485 driver.

With the FIFOs off (the course's choice), the CMSIS header names these bits with both meanings, for example USART_ISR_RXNE_RXFNE and USART_CR1_TXEIE_TXFNFIE. Unlike older STM32 families, reading the status register never clears anything: errors are cleared by writing 1 to the matching bit of USART_ICR.

8.4A lock-free ring buffer

The interrupt handler and main() run at different times and speeds. A ring buffer between them absorbs the difference. With exactly one producer and one consumer, it needs no locking: each index is written by one side only. Try it: Put is what the RX interrupt does, Get is what main() does.

⚠️
Two producers break it

The lock-free property holds only for one writer of head and one writer of tail. If two interrupts at different priorities both call ringbuf_put() on the same buffer, one can preempt the other between the read and the write of head, and a byte is lost. Then you need a critical section.

8.6LIN mode on the G4 USART

LIN (Local Interconnect Network, ISO 17987) is a cheap single-wire bus for simple car body functions: switches, mirrors, seat motors. It is a UART frame format plus a schedule: one master asks, a slave answers. The G4's USARTs (not the LPUART) have a LIN mode (USART_CR2.LINEN) that adds what a plain UART lacks (RM0440, USART):

  • Break: the master starts every frame with at least 13 low bit times, longer than any valid byte. Writing USART_RQR.SBKRQ sends one.
  • Break detection: a receiver with LINEN sets ISR.LBDF when it sees a break of 10 or 11 bits (LBDL), and can interrupt (LBDIE). Clear it with ICR.LBDCF.

A frame header is: break, sync byte 0x55 (a 10101010 pattern slaves use to measure the master's baud rate), and a protected identifier: 6 ID bits plus 2 parity bits.

Physically, LIN needs a transceiver that moves the signal to the car's 12 V level; the course keeps LIN to theory here and to a 3.3 V two-board demo on USART1 (D8/D2) in example 13. Module 12 compares LIN with CAN.
