I2C and an SSD1306 OLED

I2C connects many chips over just two wires, with addresses and an acknowledge after every byte. It is everywhere on circuit boards: displays, sensors, EEPROMs. The STM32F1's I2C peripheral is famously particular, so this module spends time on its event sequence and its errata, then drives an SSD1306 OLED.

10.1I2C on the wire

Two lines, SCL (clock, driven by the master) and SDA (data, both directions). Both are open-drain: devices can only pull a line low; a pull-up resistor (typically 4.7 kΩ) brings it back high. Any device can therefore hold a line low without short-circuiting another.

  • START: SDA falls while SCL is high. STOP: SDA rises while SCL is high. During data, SDA only changes while SCL is low.
  • The first byte is the 7-bit address plus a read/write bit. The addressed device answers with an ACK: it pulls SDA low during the ninth clock.
  • Then data bytes follow, each acknowledged by the receiver. A NACK (SDA left high) means "no such device" after the address, or "stop sending" from a master reading.
  • Standard mode runs at up to 100 kHz, fast mode up to 400 kHz (I2C specification UM10204).

10.4Errata you must know

ST's errata sheet ES096 lists several limitations of the F1 I2C peripheral. The ones that bite most often:

  • A stuck BUSY flag. If a slave is left holding SDA low (for example, the MCU reset in the middle of a read), or the analog filter latches a glitch, the peripheral believes the bus is busy forever. Software must clock the bus free and reset the peripheral.
  • Strict timing between events in master receive mode with interrupts: the sequences for reading 1, 2 or N bytes differ (RM0008 §26.3.3), and an interrupt arriving at the wrong moment can make the peripheral read one byte too many. Polling with interrupts masked for the critical steps, or DMA, avoids it.

Every wait needs a timeout

The most common I2C bug is a while (!(I2C1->SR1 & I2C_SR1_SB)); that hangs forever when the display is unplugged. Example 06 returns I2C_ERR_NACK or I2C_ERR_TIMEOUT instead, and the program keeps running.

10.5Driving the SSD1306

The SSD1306 controls a 128 × 64 pixel OLED. Its memory is organised in 8 horizontal pages of 8 pixel rows; each byte is a vertical strip of 8 pixels, bit 0 at the top. Every I2C transaction starts with a control byte: 0x00 for commands, 0x40 for display data.

Drawing happens in a 1 KB framebuffer in RAM, in exactly the controller's layout, so a flush is one long transfer. The drawing code has no hardware access and is unit-tested on your PC:

At 400 kHz a full 1024-byte frame takes about 23 ms on the wire (9 clocks per byte), so example 06 redraws about 15 times per second. Updating only the pages that changed is the usual optimisation.
