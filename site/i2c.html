I2C and an SSD1306 OLED

I2C connects many chips over just two wires, with addresses and an acknowledge after every byte. It is everywhere on circuit boards: displays, sensors, EEPROMs. The STM32G4's I2C peripheral does most of a transfer by itself once its timing register is right, so this module spends its time on that register, on the pitfalls (including one specific to this board), and then drives an SSD1306 OLED.

10.1I2C on the wire

Two lines, SCL (clock, driven by the master) and SDA (data, both directions). Both are open-drain: devices can only pull a line low; a pull-up resistor (typically 4.7 kΩ) brings it back high. Any device can therefore hold a line low without short-circuiting another.

  • START: SDA falls while SCL is high. STOP: SDA rises while SCL is high. During data, SDA only changes while SCL is low.
  • The first byte is the 7-bit address plus a read/write bit. The addressed device answers with an ACK: it pulls SDA low during the ninth clock.
  • Then data bytes follow, each acknowledged by the receiver. A NACK (SDA left high) means "no such device" after the address, or "stop sending" from a master reading.
  • Standard mode runs at up to 100 kHz, fast mode up to 400 kHz (I2C specification UM10204).

Analogy

A classroom where the teacher (master) calls a pupil by number and every pupil must raise a hand to say "heard you" after each sentence. Nobody raises a hand? Either the pupil is absent or the number was wrong, and the teacher knows immediately.

Change the address and the data and watch the transaction. Shaded bands mark the fields; ACK and NACK are the receiver's answer.

10.3The G4 I2C peripheral

The G4's I2C peripheral does most of a transfer by itself (RM0440, I2C). You tell it the slave address, the number of bytes (NBYTES, up to 255 per chunk) and whether to send STOP automatically at the end (AUTOEND), then set START. From then on it asks for each byte with TXIS and reports the outcome with STOPF or NACKF. Status flags are cleared through I2C_ICR, never by a magic read order. Step through a master write:

10.4Pitfalls: BOOT0, bus recovery and errata

⚠️
Not on D15/D14: PB8 is BOOT0

On an Arduino-style board, D15/D14 is where I2C "belongs", and on the NUCLEO-G474RE those pins are PB8/PB9 with I2C1. But PB8 is also the BOOT0 pin, sampled at every reset while the option bit nSWBOOT0 is 1 (the factory setting, module 03). The pull-up resistor on an OLED module's SCL holds PB8 high during reset, and the chip starts ST's bootloader instead of your program. Example 06 therefore uses I2C2 on PA9 (SCL, D8) and PA8 (SDA, D7), AF4 on both (DS12288).

A stuck bus. If the MCU resets in the middle of a read, a slave may be left holding SDA low, waiting for clocks that never come, and the peripheral sees the bus as busy forever. Software recovers by taking the pins over as GPIOs, clocking SCL until the slave lets go of SDA (at most nine pulses), and resetting the I2C block by clearing PE:

Errata. ST lists known limitations of each silicon revision in the device errata sheet (ES0430 for the STM32G474). Read the I2C section for your revision before you rely on corner cases such as clock stretching by slow slaves or SMBus timeouts.

⚠️
Every wait needs a timeout

The most common I2C bug is a while (!(I2C2->ISR & I2C_ISR_TXIS)); that hangs forever when the display is unplugged: TXIS never comes, NACKF does. Example 06 checks for NACKF in every wait and returns I2C_ERR_NACK or I2C_ERR_TIMEOUT instead, and the program keeps running.

10.5Driving the SSD1306

The SSD1306 controls a 128 × 64 pixel OLED. Its memory is organised in 8 horizontal pages of 8 pixel rows; each byte is a vertical strip of 8 pixels, bit 0 at the top. Every I2C transaction starts with a control byte: 0x00 for commands, 0x40 for display data.

Drawing happens in a 1 KB framebuffer in RAM, in exactly the controller's layout, so a flush is one long transfer. The drawing code has no hardware access and is unit-tested on your PC:

At 400 kHz a full 1024-byte frame takes about 23 ms on the wire (9 clocks per byte), so example 06 redraws about 15 times per second. Updating only the pages that changed is the usual optimisation.
